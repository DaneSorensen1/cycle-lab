Cycle Lab — how I'm running review for Chapters 3 and 4, and what it's showing

Dane Sorensen · ACC 153 · October 30, 2026

For the Chapter 3 and 4 review I built a practice tool instead of running review days of worked problems at the board. Students work the same kind of full problem the test asks, from the first journal entry to a balanced trial balance, on their own device, with help underneath each step rather than a solution in front of them. This note is what it does, why I built it this way, and what the first day of use is showing.

The problem it practices is the one the unit builds toward: a month of a small business’s activity, recorded event by event, carried to the right accounts, adjusted at month end for things like rent paid ahead or wages earned but not yet paid, and closed out with a trial balance whose two columns must equal. The transferable skill underneath is reducing a messy situation to the same few questions every time.

Students do the work; they do not read it. Every decision is a dropdown choice: which account, which side, what amount. Feedback is aimed at the exact slip rather than at the correct answer: if both accounts are right but swapped, it asks which account went up; if the mistake is a classic one, like recording one month of rent when the whole payment was made, it names that. Help comes in layers the student controls: the clue words light up, then which accounts and why, then the full reasoning, and only after that the answer. The questions students use to check themselves are the two rules and three questions from class, on every screen, so the success criteria are the ones students already use.

There are two companies. The first lets a student open the hints before trying; the second withholds all help until the student has tried, and says so up front, because the point of the second pass is to find out what they can do alone. A third option lets a student invent a company and watch the tool record and balance it. Students who need support use the layers and students who do not skip them, which is where the differentiation lives.

How I know. The tool keeps a record of what each student did: whether an item was solved on the first try, after a hint, or shown. My side of it answers three questions, for the class or for one block at a time. Who has finished what. Which situation trips the most students and what mistake it signals. And who I should talk to, with an opening question for each.

What it is showing after one day. Forty-two students have signed in. Thirty-six have recorded the full month of transactions and six have finished the first company through the trial balance. The entries that catch students are the ones where timing decides the answer: paying wages already earned, and buying equipment or taking repair jobs on account, each right on the first try for about seven in ten students, against nine in ten for a plain cash sale. Posting to Cash is the weakest step so far, right on the first check about half the time, and the balance most often wrong is Deferred Revenue. That is the warm-up for the next class. The record also names a handful of students who were guessing through the dropdowns rather than reasoning; I will sit with each of them for two entries.

The publisher’s online homework system can check answers, but it does not explain them in the words we use in class, and it does not tell me where the room is stuck. This runs free in a browser on a Chromebook or a phone, asks only for a first name and block, and records only what students do inside it.

It is a first version and I expect to adjust it after the first week. Whether to attach a small amount of extra credit to finishing it is still open; if I do, it will go to evidence of work rather than completion, since the record shows the difference.

I think of this as class continued at home: the same questions and the same rules, with help available when a student needs it and out of the way when they do not.
